    About the data

    What you’re looking at

    Every registered voter in New York City belongs to one election district, a small area of a few hundred to a couple thousand voters. This map counts voters in each one by party, then adds them up into City Council, State Assembly, State Senate and Congressional districts.

    Sources

    • Residents: U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2020–2024 5-year estimates, census tracts; 2020 Census blocks for the split.
    • Voter counts: New York State Board of Elections, Voter Enrollment by Election District, Party Affiliation and Status. Voters registered as of February 20, 2026. 5,359,346 voters in 4,026 election districts.
    • Election district lines: NYC Open Data, Election Districts (export of October 21, 2026).
    • Council, Assembly, Senate and Congressional lines: NYC Department of City Planning, current lines drawn after the 2020 Census.
    • Street map: © OpenStreetMap contributors, © CARTO. Address search: NYC Planning Labs GeoSearch.

    Who lives here: Census data

    Resident numbers come from the U.S. Census Bureau’s American Community Survey, 2020–2024 five-year estimates, for New York City’s 2,327 census tracts. They cover citizenship and eligible voters, age, race and ethnicity, origin groups, languages spoken at home, income, poverty, work, housing costs and education.

    Tracts and election districts don’t line up, so each tract is split using 2020 Census blocks, the smallest Census areas. A block’s residents go to the election district its center falls in. Person counts follow where people lived in 2020; household counts follow housing units. Districts are then the sum of their election districts. Median income for anything bigger than a tract is estimated from income brackets. When you view census tracts, voter counts move the other way, from election districts to tracts.

    Margins of error and reliability

    Every census number comes with a 90% margin of error, shown as ± in the comparison table and in downloads. Margins for districts are built from the tract margins the Census Bureau publishes, using the Bureau’s own approximation. Each number is also rated:

    • High: the margin is small next to the estimate (coefficient of variation under 15%).
    • Medium: 15% to 30%, or the area gets most of its residents from tracts that had to be split. Every single election district falls here or lower.
    • Low: over 30%, an estimate of zero, or more than 130 active voters per 100 eligible, which signals the split is off for that area. Low numbers are faded on the map.

    Margins describe survey sampling. They don’t include the extra error from splitting tracts. Median income for areas larger than a tract is estimated from income brackets and has no margin.

    How we checked the data

    • Every voter count matches the Board of Elections workbooks cell for cell and adds up to each county’s published total.
    • Every election district’s Council, Assembly, Senate and Congress assignment matches the Census Bureau’s and NYC Open Data’s own district lines. The only disagreements are five slivers with no voters.
    • Census tract numbers add up exactly to the Bureau’s published county and citywide figures.
    • For the 102 Senate, Assembly and Congressional districts entirely inside NYC, the Bureau publishes its own district estimates. Ours match them: population within 2.5% (usually within 0.6%), every share tested within about 2 points, and our margins of error within a few percent of the Bureau’s.
    • The website’s math is tested against an independent build: 863,850 values and margins, zero differences.
    • Address lookup puts every populated 2020 census block in the same election district and tract as the full-detail boundaries.

    This is built to tell a story, not to certify a number. Survey estimates for single tracts and small groups have wide margins of error, and the split assumes people are spread the way they were in 2020. “Active voters per 100 eligible” compares February 2026 registration with 2020–2024 citizen counts, so it can run over 100 in places that grew or have older voter rolls.

    How districts are added up

    Every election district sits inside a single district of each type, so each district’s total is the exact sum of the election districts inside it. Nothing is estimated or split.

      Things to know

      • Party names: “No party” means voters not enrolled in any party (listed as BLANK by the Board). “Other parties” combines every party besides Democratic, Republican, Conservative and Working Families.
      • Address search uses simplified district lines. For an address right on a district border, confirm with the city’s poll site locator.
      • Election district 40047 in Queens (1 voter) has no published boundary. It counts toward Assembly 40 and Queens only.
      • 188 mapped election districts have no registered voters. They are parks, airports and similar areas, shown in gray.
